# Jason Arday is now the stick the right will use to beat back racial equality. Don’t let that happen | Afua Hirsch

### Key takeaways

In 1987, a college at the University of Cambridge considered offering a prestigious position to a black man in what would be the first appointment of its kind. Not only did the man not have a degree, but he had left school at the age of 13, struggling with dyslexia and – at the time – unable to read or write. That he would later to go on to accumulate 20 honorary doctorates and become a professor of creative writing is only one way, and in my view not the best one, of measuring his talent.

The man was the late, great poet Benjamin Zephaniah. And he had been nothing but transparent about his lack of credentials. In fact it was his background that made him eligible for this fellowship, which was designed to reach out to somebody in the community who didn’t have a degree, to give them the opportunity to have a connection with the university.

Zephaniah had been privately offered the role but a formal offer had not yet been made when it was leaked to the rightwing press. The Sun and the Daily Mail newspapers enjoyed a feeding frenzy at the prospect of a black man – one with locs, no less – becoming a fellow at Cambridge.

“Would you let this man near your daughter?” asked the Sun. “Just what are his qualities which have appealed to Trinity College?” the paper asked. Then, not bothering to hide its starkly racist views, it answered that there were none. No qualities, only problems, listed in order of severity: “He is black. He is a Rastafarian. He has tasted approved schools and Borstals.”

The Sun offered no evidence whatsoever for the implication that Zephaniah was a rapist, as the headline suggested. Apparently none was needed – the audience already understood that a black man from Handsworth, Birmingham, who had “tasted Borstals” could not possibly be an appropriate or safe person to be associated with Cambridge University.

In the years since Zephaniah did not receive the fellowship that should have been his due, Cambridge – like many elite universities – has been mired in actual scandals of rape, nepotism, plagiarism and more. Remember them? You probably don’t, because they barely made the news.

For a period beginning in 2019, Trinity Hall, Cambridge, was embroiled in controversy after it emerged that not one but three people who worked at the college had been accused of sexual misconduct by students. The ensuing outcry led the master to resign.

In 2023, a professor from the same college was allowed to keep his job after having been found to have plagiarised material from a student essay.

In 2015, a row broke out in which the renowned University of Leeds sociologist Zygmunt Bauman was accused of multiple instances of “self-plagiarism”. Colleagues claimed those allegations were motivated by another scholar’s “personal agenda” and said they were “tantamount to a form of intellectual violence wrapped in objective scholarship that plagues the academy”.

Maybe it’s just my algorithm, but social media seems flooded with hot takes on Jason Arday. I have worked with him – we were developing a project together at one point – and I have thought a lot about him in the years since.

The fact is that many of Arday’s black peers in academic sociology have been voicing concerns about him for a while. He seemed, to them, to have come out of nowhere. His performance at high-profile academic events did not match the credentials that were being touted. I have had various conversations with sociology professors in which this has been the main topic of conversation, and their questions began to mount into a list. They did not voice them publicly because we all know that whatever the truth or the outcome, he would be unlikely to be treated fairly and we would all be damaged in the process. As for me, I was an interested observer to allegations the veracity of which I couldn’t personally verify. But hearing them from fellow black academics I respect was enough to put me on alert and raise a red flag for anyone who asked me whether publicly supporting him was a good idea.
