# How connected motors, drives and monitoring technologies turn industrial energy data into operational advantages

Energy data brings a genuine competitive edge The industrial sector accounted for nearly 40 per cent of global final energy consumption in 2024, according to the International Energy Agency (IEA)’s Energy Efficiency 2025 report, the largest share of any end user sector.

Industrial operators must therefore reduce energy consumption without compromising productivity. One answer lies in better use of data.

Here, Marek Lukaszczyk, marketing manager for WEG UK and Middle East, explains why energy and asset-performance data offers a competitive edge in motor and drive operations.

For decades, energy efficiency in industrial environments was often viewed through the lens of equipment replacement.

An engineer would identify an inefficient motor in an application like a pump or compressor, calculate the likely payback period and decide whether the investment could be justified.

While this approach still has value, it is no longer enough. Today, energy use is not just a cost line, but a real-time indicator of site performance.

Rising consumption may point to overloaded equipment, poor process control, failing components or inefficient maintenance.

Companies that understand their energy and asset data are better placed to reduce waste, protect uptime and make informed investment decisions. This matters because industrial efficiency progress is slowing.

The IEA’s Energy Efficiency 2025 report also found that industry has contributed two-thirds of the total increase in global final energy demand since 2019.

Put simply, global energy efficiency progress has not been fast enough to meet long-term climate and competitiveness goals.

Take, for instance, electric motor-driven systems which power the pumps, fans, compressors, conveyors, mixers and process equipment that keep industrial operations moving.

The United Nations Industrial Development Organization (UNIDO) reports that electric motor systems account for about 60 per cent of global industrial electricity consumption and close to 70 per cent of industrial electricity demand.

While these figures are alarming, they also point to major opportunities. For manufacturers, even small improvements in how electric motor systems are monitored and managed can have a significant impact.

From measurement to action Simply collecting data does not create a competitive advantage. The value lies in turning that data into timely action.

In many plants, energy information is still reviewed retrospectively through utility bills, audits or dashboard summaries.

These tools show consumption trends after the fact, but not always why energy use changed, which asset was responsible or what action should be taken next.

To create meaningful operational value, energy data must be connected to asset-performance data. This means understanding how motors, drives and rotating equipment behave under real operating conditions.

If a motor is consuming more energy than expected, is it because demand has increased, the process has changed, the drive is poorly configured or a mechanical problem is emerging?

Without this context, energy data remains incomplete. Digital monitoring technologies are playing an increasingly important role in asset management.

Online monitoring systems can be used to track the condition of low and medium voltage motors and drives, as well as associated equipment such as gearboxes, gearmotors, compressors, pumps and fans.

These systems typically combine sensors attached to electric motors with cloud-based monitoring platforms.

The sensors collect data such as vibration, temperature and running hours, helping operators assess asset condition, monitor performance remotely and identify potential faults.

Crucially, the economics of deployment are also changing: this technology is now available at lower costs than in the past, making continuous monitoring a more realistic option for a wider range of industrial sites.

The cost of downtime The business case for better monitoring is not limited to energy savings. It also relates directly to uptime. Unplanned downtime remains one of the most expensive risks in industrial operations.

4 trillion US dollars annually through unplanned downtime, equivalent to 11 per cent of their revenues. This is why energy and asset data should not be treated as separate disciplines.

A motor that is running inefficiently may also be at greater risk of failure. A pump operating away from its best efficiency point may consume more electricity while placing additional stress on mechanical components.

A fan or compressor with rising vibration levels may continue operating, but at reduced efficiency and with a growing risk of breakdown.

By monitoring these assets more closely, operators can make better decisions about maintenance, process optimisation and equipment upgrades.

In practice, this can mean lubricating a bearing at the right time, adjusting a drive profile, replacing an inefficient motor or identifying a recurring fault before it affects production.

Preventing long-term damage To demonstrate how this works in practice, let’s look to an example from the cement industry.

A raw mill elevator drive, a critical asset used to elevate raw product to the top of a mill house, was fitted with WEGscan wireless monitoring technology.

If the drive failed, production would stop, creating downtime, economic losses and potential disruption to downstream processes.

Three days after installation, the system detected abnormal vibration at the non-drive end of the motor. Over six hours, vibration increased significantly, triggering an alert to the maintenance team.

Analysis indicated a dry bearing condition, prompting lubrication. After greasing, the acceleration trend returned to baseline. The significance lies in the timing.

Previously, the asset had been checked monthly using portable vibration equipment, meaning the short-duration spike may have been missed.

By detecting the issue in real time, WEGscan enabled early intervention and helped prevent long-term damage.

A dry bearing is not only a maintenance issue; it can also increase friction, reduce mechanical efficiency and strain the drive system.

Across a plant’s motors, pumps, fans and compressors, these hidden inefficiencies can become a significant operational cost.

Connecting efficiency and reliability Energy efficiency and reliability have sometimes been treated as separate objectives.

Plant managers focused on output, maintenance teams focused on keeping equipment running, and sustainability teams focused on reducing consumption and emissions.

A high-efficiency motor can reduce energy consumption, but its long-term value depends on how well it is applied, controlled and maintained.

A variable speed drive can reduce wasted energy by matching motor output to process demand, but its benefits depend on correct configuration and process insight.

A condition monitoring system can detect early signs of failure, but its greatest value comes when the data is used to inform wider operational decisions. This is where a more integrated approach is needed.

By combining efficient motors, variable speed drives and digital monitoring, manufacturers can move from one-off efficiency projects to continuous improvement. Energy data shows where consumption is occurring.

Asset data explains why it is happening. Control technologies allow operators to act on that insight. The next five years Over the next five years, energy data will become a more central part of everyday production management.

Rather than being reviewed only by energy managers or sustainability teams, it will increasingly be used by maintenance engineers, plant managers and operations teams to make real-time decisions.

This shift will be driven by three factors. First, pressure to reduce energy consumption and emissions will continue to grow. Second, energy-intensive manufacturers will need greater resilience against cost volatility.

Third, digital technologies will make it easier to collect, analyse and act on data from individual assets across an entire site. The most competitive industrial operators will be those that can connect these areas.

They will not look at energy consumption in isolation, nor will they treat maintenance as a purely reactive function.

Instead, they will use data from motors, drives and rotating equipment to understand how efficiently their processes are running, where risk is building and where investment will deliver the greatest return.

Energy data is therefore becoming more than a reporting tool. Used correctly, it can support lower operating costs, stronger sustainability performance and improved productivity.
